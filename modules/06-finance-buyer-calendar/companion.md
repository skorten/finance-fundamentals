## 1. Learning goals

After this module you should be able to:

1. Find a company's fiscal year end, and place its budget season, close periods and audit on a calendar.
2. Tell the CFO, the controller and FP&A apart, and know which questions belong to each.
3. Use the finance terms from this course correctly with a finance buyer.

## 2. Who the finance buyers are

Finance buyers are not one person. Three roles show up on most deals, and each one reads your pitch through a different question.

| Role | Owns | Typical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CFO | Strategy, investors, the board | How does this change our margin and our story to investors? |
| Controller | Accounting accuracy, the close, the audit | Will this number hold up at close and in the audit? |
| FP&A | Planning, forecasting, variance | Which team or customer caused the variance, and what does it do to the forecast? |

The typical questions are illustrations drawn from Modules 2 to 5. Titles and duties vary by company. A small company may have one person doing two of these jobs. A large one may split each job across a team. Confirm who owns what on every deal.

**Inference:** a good opening question for any new finance contact is "Who owns this on your side, and what does their month look like?" It tells you who the real buyer is and when they have time.

## 3. Datadog's calendar, from its 10-K

Datadog is a public company, so its calendar is written down in its filings. That makes it a good practice prospect.

**Fiscal year.** The 10-K cover page says "For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2025." Datadog's fiscal year is the calendar year. Its quarters end on March 31, June 30, September 30 and December 31.

**Audit.** The audit finishes after year end. Deloitte & Touche LLP, Datadog's auditor since 2016, dated its audit reports February 18, 2026. Datadog filed the 10-K with the SEC the same day. About seven weeks separate year end from the filing.

**SOX.** The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) shows up in the same filing in three ways:

1. Management reports that its internal control over financial reporting "was effective as of December 31, 2025."
2. Deloitte issued a separate report on those controls.
3. The principal executive officer and principal financial officer each sign certifications under Sections 302 and 906 of the Act. These are filed as exhibits.

**Seller's view of budget timing.** Datadog reports that it signs "a higher percentage of subscription agreements with new customers and renewals with existing customers in the fourth quarter." It adds: "We believe that this results from the procurement, budgeting, and deployment cycles of many of our customers, particularly our enterprise customers." That is a public company telling investors that customer budget cycles move its own sales. It is useful, confirmed evidence that budget timing is real.

**What the 10-K does not say.** It does not say how long each month end or quarter end close takes. It does not say when Datadog's own budget season runs. Those are company specific. Treat any dates beyond the filed facts as inference and confirm them with the prospect.

## 4. The calendar picture (slides 4 to 6)

**Close.** After each month and quarter ends, the accounting team spends working days finalizing the books. Year end close is the heaviest, and the audit runs on top of it. For a December year end company, that means January and the first half of February are crowded. The quarter ends in March, June and September start a lighter version of the same crunch in the first weeks of April, July and October.

**Budget season.** Module 5 covered budgets. The calendar point is when budgets get set. A common rule of thumb is that budget season runs in the two to four months before the fiscal year starts. For a January 1 start, that means roughly September to December. **Inference:** this rule comes from the course outline. It is not in the Datadog 10-K and it is not a law. Companies differ. Confirm the actual timing with the prospect.

**Why it matters.** A new spending request that arrives after the budget is set may wait a year, or need an exception. A business case that arrives while the budget is being shaped has a path. This is why the deck's heat strip (slide 6) shows budget season as its own row.

**The heat strip, explained.** Slide 6 is illustrative. It marks January, February, April, July and October as months with close or audit work. That is five of twelve. It marks September to December as budget season. Months with no close work are labeled "open." Open does not mean a CFO is waiting for your call. It means the books are not the reason to say no. The strip applies to a December fiscal year only. If your prospect's year starts in February or July, shift every band.

**How to find a prospect's fiscal year end.** For a public company, read the cover page of the 10-K. It states the fiscal year end. The Investor.gov guide to reading a 10-K shows where the pieces sit. For a private company, ask.

## 5. How a CFO hears it

**Timing.** *Inference:* the controller's team is at full stretch during each close, and most of all during year end close and the audit. A meeting request that lands in those weeks competes with the books. Budget season is when next year's spending gets approved, so a proposal that arrives after the budget is set may wait a year or need an exception.

A bad timing example from slide 9: you ask a controller for a meeting about cloud costs in the middle of the audit. You meant to be helpful. The controller hears that you do not know what their team is doing right now. That is an inference about how the message lands, not a measured result.

**Vocabulary.** *Inference:* vocabulary signals whether an SE understands the finance buyer's job. Three swaps help:

- Say "cost of revenue" for the production cloud bill, not "hosting spend." (Module 2)
- Say "variance against budget" for overspend, not "we went over." (Module 5)
- Say which line on which statement moves, not "we save money." (Module 1)

Each one matches the language in the buyer's own reports. That is the point. You are not showing off. You are making it easy for them to repeat your message to their boss.

## 6. Showback and chargeback

Both terms describe what a company does after it splits a shared bill among internal teams.

- **Showback** reports each team's share to that team. Nobody pays. It is a report.
- **Chargeback** charges each team's budget for its share. Money moves between internal budgets. It is closer to an invoice.

Slide 11 draws showback as a report with one bar per team and chargeback as a debit against each team's budget.

**Who owns it?** *Inference:* chargeback is an internal allocation decision, so FP&A usually leads. The controller usually owns how it is recorded in the books. Ownership varies by company, and Emily's review should confirm this split.

**Why the difference matters in a sales call.** A team that wants showback needs good reports. A team that wants chargeback needs numbers solid enough to bill against, which means the controller will want to trust the allocation rules. Ask which one the prospect wants before you demo.

## 8. Self-check exercise 1: build a prospect calendar (5 minutes)

Treat Datadog as the prospect. Using the facts above, answer these questions. The deck asks question 4 as a multiple choice (slide 15). Try all four on paper first.

1. When does Datadog's fiscal year start and end?
2. In which months would you expect quarter end close work?
3. When did year end close and the audit wrap up for FY2025?
4. Using the rule of thumb that budget season runs in the two to four months before the fiscal year starts, when would you expect Datadog's budget season?

### Answers

1. It runs January 1 to December 31.
2. In the first weeks of January, April, July and October, after each quarter ends. The exact length of each close is company specific and is not in the 10-K.
3. The audit reports and the 10-K are dated February 18, 2026. **Inference:** year end close and the audit took up January and the first half of February.
4. Starting between September and early November and running to year end, ahead of a January 1 start. **Inference:** the two to four month rule comes from the course outline and is not in the 10-K. Confirm the actual timing with the prospect.

**Slide 15 answer:** A. September to year end.

## 9. Self-check exercise 2: match the question to the buyer (5 minutes)

Decide whether each question belongs first to the CFO, the controller or FP&A. The deck asks question 1 (slide 16).

1. "Cloud cost came in $72K over budget last quarter. Which teams caused it?"
2. "If we capitalize this work, what documentation will the auditors want?"
3. "What does a point of gross margin mean for our valuation?"
4. "Can we charge each business unit for its share of shared infrastructure?"

The $72K figure is hypothetical. It repeats the Module 5 exercise.

### Answers

1. **FP&A.** It is a variance question (Module 5).
2. **Controller.** It concerns accounting policy and audit support (Module 3).
3. **CFO.** It concerns investors and strategy (Module 2).
4. **FP&A first, with the controller involved.** *Inference:* chargeback is an internal allocation decision, and the controller usually owns how it is recorded. Ownership varies by company.

## 10. Vocabulary glossary for this module

- **Fiscal year:** a company's 12 month accounting year. It may or may not match the calendar year.
- **Budget cycle:** the months before the fiscal year starts, when next year's budget is set (Module 5).
- **Month end and quarter end close:** the working days after a period ends, when the accounting team finalizes that period's books.
- **Audit:** the external auditor's examination of the annual financial statements.
- **SOX:** the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002. It sets internal control and certification rules for US public companies.
- **Internal control over financial reporting:** the processes that make reported numbers reliable. SOX Section 404 requires management to assess them every year.
- **Showback:** reporting each internal team's share of a cost to that team, without billing it.
- **Chargeback:** charging each internal team's budget for its share of a cost.
- **CFO:** the chief financial officer. Owns strategy, investor relations and the board's view of the numbers.
- **Controller:** the head of accounting. Owns accuracy, the close and the audit.
- **FP&A:** financial planning and analysis. The team that plans, forecasts and explains variance.
- **10-K:** a public company's audited annual report, filed with the SEC.
- **Variance:** actual minus budget (Module 5).
